4.2 - Triangle Congruence: SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, and HL

Build a square from four sticks and four bolts. Push on one corner. The square tilts. Now build a triangle from three sticks. Push on it. It does not move.

Three sides lock a triangle into one shape. This is why bridges, roofs, and bike frames are built from triangles. In this lesson you find out which three parts are enough to know that two triangles are congruent.

SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, HL, included angle, included side

4.2.1 - Why Triangles? SSS

  • A triangle with three fixed side lengths can have only one shape. A four-sided frame can tilt.
  • SSS (side, side, side): If three sides of one triangle are congruent to three sides of another triangle, the triangles are congruent.
  • SSS is the rule behind the distance check on a grid in 4.1.

Example 1: Use SSS

Are the triangles congruent? Write a congruence statement.

The marks show \(AB \cong YZ\) (one tick), \(BC \cong ZX\) (two ticks), and \(AC \cong YX\) (three ticks).

All three pairs of sides are congruent. By SSS, \(\triangle ABC \cong\) \(\triangle YZX\).

The letters follow the marks: \(A\) matches \(Y\), \(B\) matches \(Z\), and \(C\) matches \(X\).

Yes, by SSS. \(\triangle PQR \cong \triangle STU\).

A bridge truss, a roof truss, and a bike frame are made of triangles. A triangle cannot be pushed out of shape. A rectangle can.

4.2.2 - SAS, ASA, AAS, and HL

  • SAS (side, angle, side): two sides and the angle between them. The angle between two sides is the included angle.
  • ASA (angle, side, angle): two angles and the side between them. The side between two angles is the included side.
  • AAS (angle, angle, side): two angles and a side that is not between them.
  • HL (hypotenuse, leg): works for right triangles only. The hypotenuse and one leg are congruent.

Example 2: Use SAS

The marks show \(AB \cong DE\), \(AC \cong DF\), and \(\angle A \cong \angle D\).

Angle \(A\) is between \(\overline{AB}\) and \(\overline{AC}\). Angle \(D\) is between \(\overline{DE}\) and \(\overline{DF}\).

Two sides and the included angle match. By SAS, \(\triangle ABC \cong\) \(\triangle DEF\).

Example 3: Use AAS

The marks show \(\angle G \cong \angle J\), \(\angle H \cong \angle K\), and \(HI \cong KL\).

Side \(\overline{HI}\) is not between \(\angle G\) and \(\angle H\). It touches \(\angle H\) and \(\angle I\).

Two angles and a side that is not between them match. By AAS, \(\triangle GHI \cong\) \(\triangle JKL\).

Example 4: Use HL

The figure shows right angles at \(A\) and \(D\). The hypotenuses \(\overline{BC}\) and \(\overline{EF}\) are congruent. The legs \(\overline{AC}\) and \(\overline{DF}\) are congruent.

Both triangles are right triangles, and the hypotenuse and a leg match. By HL, \(\triangle ABC \cong\) \(\triangle DEF\).

  1. In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle DEF\), \(\angle A \cong \angle D\), \(AB \cong DE\), and \(\angle B \cong \angle E\). Which rule shows the triangles are congruent?

  2. In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle DEF\), \(AB \cong DE\), \(BC \cong EF\), and \(\angle B \cong \angle E\). Which rule?

  1. ASA. Side \(\overline{AB}\) is between \(\angle A\) and \(\angle B\).

  2. SAS. Angle \(B\) is between \(\overline{AB}\) and \(\overline{BC}\).

4.2.3 - What Is Not Enough: SSA and AAA

  • SSA (two sides and an angle that is not between them) is not enough. Two different triangles can have these parts.
  • AAA (three angles) is not enough. The triangles have the same shape but can have different sizes.
  • A counterexample is one example that shows a rule does not always work.

Example 5: A counterexample for SSA

In the figure, \(AB = 5\), \(BC = BD = 3.2\), and \(\angle A = 30^\circ\) in both triangles. Two sides and a non-included angle match.

But \(AC\) is about 2.3 and \(AD\) is about 6.3. The triangles are not congruent.

So SSA does not show that triangles are congruent.

Example 6: A counterexample for AAA

Both triangles have angles \(40^\circ\), \(60^\circ\), and \(80^\circ\). The larger triangle has sides that are 2 times as long.

The angles match, but the sizes are different. The triangles are not congruent. AAA does not work.

No. The angles match (AAA), but AAA is not enough. The sizes are different.

If the marks give only SSA or AAA, the triangles might be congruent. You just cannot tell from those facts. Write “not enough information”.

4.2.4 - Which Shortcut? Use the Marks and the Given Facts

  • Use only the marks and the facts you are given. A picture can look congruent and not be.
  • A side that both triangles share is congruent to itself. It counts as one pair of sides.
  • Vertical angles are equal (1.3). Parallel lines make equal alternate interior angles (1.4).
  • Write the congruence statement with the letters in matching order. If no rule fits, write “not enough information”.

Example 7: A shared side

\(AB \cong AD\) and \(BC \cong DC\). Are \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle ADC\) congruent?

Both triangles share \(\overline{AC}\), so \(AC \cong AC\).

Three pairs of sides are congruent. By SSS, \(\triangle ABC \cong\) \(\triangle ADC\).

Example 8: Vertical angles

\(\overline{AD}\) and \(\overline{BC}\) cross at \(E\). \(AE \cong DE\) and \(BE \cong CE\). Which rule shows \(\triangle AEB \cong \triangle DEC\)?

\(\angle AEB\) and \(\angle DEC\) are vertical angles, so they are equal.

Two sides and the included angle match. By SAS, \(\triangle AEB \cong\) \(\triangle DEC\).

Example 9: Parallel lines

\(\overline{AB} \parallel \overline{DC}\) and \(AB \cong DC\). Which rule shows \(\triangle ABD \cong \triangle CDB\)?

\(\overline{BD}\) is a shared side. Because \(\overline{AB} \parallel \overline{DC}\), the alternate interior angles \(\angle ABD\) and \(\angle CDB\) are equal.

Two sides and the included angle match. By SAS, \(\triangle ABD \cong\) \(\triangle CDB\).

Example 10: Not enough information

The triangles look the same. The marks show \(AB \cong DE\), \(BC \cong EF\), and \(\angle A \cong \angle D\).

Angle \(A\) is not between \(\overline{AB}\) and \(\overline{BC}\). This is SSA.

SSA is not enough. The answer is: not enough information.

The angles at \(T\) are vertical angles, so they are equal. Two sides and the included angle match: SAS.

4.2.5 - Solve for \(x\) in Congruent Triangles

  • First use the marks to decide that the triangles are congruent.
  • Then matching parts are congruent. Set them equal and solve.

Example 11: Find \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\)

\(AC \cong DF\), \(BC \cong EF\), and \(\angle C \cong \angle F\). Find \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), and \(AB\).

Two sides and the included angle match. By SAS, \(\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF\).

\(BC = EF\), so \(2y + 3 = 15\) and $y = $ 6.

\(\angle C \cong \angle F\), so \(5z + 1 = 61\) and $z = $ 12.

\(\overline{AB}\) matches \(\overline{DE}\), so \(3x + 1 = 5x - 9\). Then \(10 = 2x\) and $x = $ 5.

$AB = 3(5) + 1 = $ 16

\(4x - 7 = 2x + 9\), so \(x = 8\). \(y + 5 = 3y - 7\), so \(y = 6\). \(BC = 11\).
